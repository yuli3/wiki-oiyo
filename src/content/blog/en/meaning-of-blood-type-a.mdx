<HighlightBox type="abstract" title="Definition">
  **Blood Type A personality** is a popular folk stereotype in Korea and Japan,
  where Type A people are often described as careful, earnest, organized, and
  reserved. This is cultural folklore and entertainment only: ABO blood type has
  **no reliable scientific support** as a way to measure personality.
</HighlightBox>

## The folk stereotype

The Type A stereotype says that people with Type A blood are careful,
responsible, sincere, and attentive to rules or social expectations. In casual
Korean and Japanese pop culture, Type A may be portrayed as the person who plans
ahead, notices details, keeps promises, and tries not to inconvenience others.

The same stereotype sometimes adds a more difficult side: reserved emotions,
perfectionism, worry, or hesitation to speak openly. These are popularly
believed associations, not traits that follow from blood biology. A Type A
person can be spontaneous, messy, bold, private, sociable, anxious, relaxed, or
anything else. The label is a cultural script, not evidence about an individual.

## Where it comes from

Modern blood-type personality claims are usually traced to early 20th-century
Japan. Takeji Furukawa, a Japanese educator, published writing in the 1920s
that attempted to connect blood type and temperament. Those claims did not
become accepted science, but the idea remained culturally memorable.

The belief was later popularized through books, magazines, television, manga,
dating culture, and celebrity profiles, especially after Masahiko Nomi's
publications in the 1970s. Korea then adopted and adapted the idea as a light
social category. For the broader context, see the
[blood-type personality definition](/en/meaning-of-blood-type/).

## Why it is not science

Large studies and critical reviews have not found a reliable relationship
between ABO blood type and personality. For example, research by Kengo Nawata
using large-scale survey data in Japan and the United States reported no
meaningful association, and other studies have failed to support the common
folk claims.

The stereotype can still feel accurate because broad descriptions invite the
Barnum effect, confirmation bias, and selective memory. People may remember a
careful Type A friend and ignore Type A people who do not match the script.

There is also a social risk. In Japan, blood-type harassment is sometimes
called **bura-hara**, short for blood-type harassment. The label can become a
source of teasing, exclusion, dating pressure, or workplace bias.

Blood Type A personality is entertainment and cultural folklore only. It is not
science, psychology, diagnosis, or a basis for judging people, hiring, school
decisions, friendship, dating, marriage, or any serious relationship choice.
