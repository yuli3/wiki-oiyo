**Asian dust describes an emission and transport phenomenon; PM10 and PM2.5 are particle-size ranges; photochemical smog involves atmospheric chemistry, including ground-level ozone.** These can occur together. A simple opposition between natural dust and artificial fine particles confuses different classification axes. Neither sky colour nor one concentration reading identifies the full source mixture or an individual's risk.

## 1. Dust emission and transport

The [WMO overview of sand and dust storms](https://public.wmo.int/themes/sand-and-dust-storms) describes strong winds lifting material from dry soils and transporting particles far from their sources. Arid regions such as the Gobi are important sources, but vegetation loss, agriculture and land management can also expose soil to wind erosion. Calling dust natural does not mean human influence or health hazards are absent.

Surface emission, lofting, transport and deposition are separate processes. Saltation is the bouncing of grains near the ground, not a process occurring within an upper-level jet stream. Heights and travel times depend on particle and weather conditions; there is no fixed altitude or two-day arrival time for every event.

Dust contains a range of particle sizes. PM10 is not a label reserved for Asian dust, and a PM2.5 measurement alone does not exclude mineral-dust contributions.

Particles can be emitted directly from soil, construction or combustion, or form through reactions in the air. [EPA's PM explanation](https://www.epa.gov/pm-pollution/particulate-matter-pm-basics) distinguishes direct emissions from secondary formation. A size label does not determine composition or source.

**Ground-level ozone is a gas**, not another name for PM2.5. [EPA's ozone explanation](https://www.epa.gov/ground-level-ozone-pollution/ground-level-ozone-basics) describes its formation through reactions involving nitrogen oxides (NOₓ), volatile organic compounds (VOC) and sunlight. Ozone concentration and particle mass concentration must be read separately.

## 3. Inversion describes mixing conditions

A temperature inversion is a layer in which temperature increases with height. On clear, calm nights, surface cooling can produce an inversion near the ground. Stable layering can suppress vertical mixing and contribute to pollutant accumulation. See the [US National Weather Service explanation](https://www.weather.gov/lmk/inversion).

This does not mean all air movement stops or that gases are simply compressed into PM2.5. Emissions, chemical formation, transport, mixing and removal are different processes. Dust transported from a source can also encounter weak dispersion in a receiving region.

Dust occurrence, particle concentration, ozone concentration and meteorological warnings are not interchangeable indicators. A yellow or grey sky alone cannot establish a particular composition or the adequacy of protective equipment.

## Summary

Source and transport ask where material came from; particle size asks what range is measured; chemistry asks what forms; inversion concerns mixing. Separating these questions allows dust and smog to coexist without contradiction.
