"The number of employed people rose by 200,000 from a year earlier" and "the perceived unemployment rate actually went up" sometimes appear in Korean news on the same day. Neither statement is wrong. This chapter starts with how employment statistics divide people up and count them.

---

## 1. Dividing people into three groups

The source of Korea's employment statistics is the **Economically Active Population Survey (경제활동인구조사)** run by 국가데이터처 (the national data and statistics agency, formerly Statistics Korea, 통계청). Sample households are surveyed every month, and the results are published in the middle of the following month as the "Employment Trends" release (「고용동향」). The base is the **population aged 15 and over**, classified by their activity during the survey reference week (the week that includes the 15th of each month).

```
Population aged 15 and over
 ├─ Economically active population
 │    ├─ Employed
 │    └─ Unemployed
 └─ Economically inactive population (students, homemakers, older people, "just rested", job preparation, etc.)
```

- **Employed**: people who worked **at least 1 hour for income** during the survey reference week. This also includes unpaid family workers who worked 18 hours or more a week in a family business, and people temporarily absent from work.
- **Unemployed**: people who had no work, **actively looked for work during the past 4 weeks**, and could start immediately if a job were available.
- **Economically inactive**: people who are neither of the above. People who have given up looking for work, or who are only preparing for employment, fall into this group.

---

## 2. The unemployment rate and the employment rate use different denominators

| Indicator | Calculation | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| Unemployment rate | Unemployed ÷ economically active population × 100 | When people give up looking, they drop out of the unemployed, so **the unemployment rate can actually fall** |
| Employment rate | Employed ÷ population aged 15 and over × 100 | Less affected by people giving up the job search. OECD comparisons use the employment rate for ages **15 to 64** |
| Labour force participation rate | Economically active population ÷ population aged 15 and over × 100 | Share of people who are working or looking for work |

As a simple example, if out of 100 people aged 15 and over, 60 are employed, 2 are unemployed and 38 are economically inactive, the unemployment rate is 2 ÷ 62 ≈ **3.2%** and the employment rate is 60 ÷ 100 = **60%**. If those 2 unemployed people stop looking for work, the unemployment rate becomes 0%, but the employment rate stays at 60%. That is why things can look better if you only look at the unemployment rate.

---

## 3. People the unemployment rate misses: supplementary employment indicators

To cover people the official unemployment rate misses, **supplementary employment indicators (고용보조지표)** are published alongside it.

- **Supplementary indicator 1**: unemployed + **time-related underemployed** (people working fewer than 36 hours a week who want to work more)
- **Supplementary indicator 2**: unemployed + **potential labour force** (people who want to work but did not look for a job, or who looked but could not start right away)
- **Supplementary indicator 3**: the sum of both of the above. It is often called the **extended unemployment rate** or the perceived unemployment rate (체감실업률).

The gap between the official unemployment rate and supplementary indicator 3 is especially wide among young people. News stories saying "the unemployment rate is low but it feels bad" are usually about this gap.

---

## 4. The order to read the employment release

1. **Change in the number of employed (year on year)**: comparing the same month removes seasonal factors.
2. **Change by age group**: if people aged 60 and over increase while young people and people in their 40s decrease, the quality differs even if the total looks good.
3. **Change by industry**: whether jobs rose or fell in manufacturing, construction, or accommodation and food services.
4. **Employment rate (ages 15 to 64) and the extended unemployment rate**: these two show the trend more honestly than the unemployment rate.
5. **The "just rested" (쉬었음) population**: people who are neither ill nor unwilling to work but are simply resting. A rise is read as a sign of people giving up the job search.

<Callout type="important" title="How US employment data differ">
The United States publishes a household survey (the unemployment rate) and an establishment survey (**Nonfarm Payrolls**) separately. Markets pay more attention to the job changes in the establishment survey. Chapter 4 covers this.
</Callout>

## Summary

Employment statistics divide the population aged 15 and over into the employed, the unemployed and the economically inactive. Because the unemployment rate can fall when people give up looking, read it together with the employment rate and the extended unemployment rate, and check the quality through changes by age and industry.
