The most common confusion when taking out the trash is the line between **"food waste or general waste?"** Because it came off the dinner table, it is easy to assume it is food waste, but many items actually belong in the regular volume-based bag.

One rule decides it: **"Can this be recycled into animal feed or farm compost?"**

---

## 1. How food waste is recycled

Food waste collected in South Korea goes to public recycling facilities and is processed in three main ways.

1. **Dry animal feed**: Water is removed and the waste is sterilized at high temperature, then used as a protein and organic ingredient in mixed feed.
2. **Composting**: Mixed with sawdust and fermented aerobically into organic fertilizer.
3. **Biogasification (anaerobic digestion)**: Broken down by microbes without oxygen to extract methane for power generation.

All three assume the waste **"won't break the machinery (high-speed shredders and dryers) and won't harm livestock digestion."** So residue that is too hard, or has no nutritional value, can't be food waste.

Even though they come from food, residues in these four groups must go in the **regular volume-based bag**.

### (1) Animal bones and shellfish shells
- **Items**: beef, pork, chicken and duck bones; thick fish bones; clam, mussel, oyster and cockle shells; crab and crayfish shells
- **Why**: They wear down shredder blades and are a main cause of machine stoppages. Bone or shell fragments in feed can pierce an animal's gullet and stomach.

### (2) Hard fruit pits and nut shells
- **Items**: peach, apricot, plum, persimmon and cherry pits; walnut, chestnut, peanut and acorn shells; coconut husks
- **Why**: They are hard and woody, so they barely ferment and don't grind down in shredders.

### (3) Tough fibrous or pungent vegetable scraps
- **Items**: onion, garlic, ginger and corn husks; corn silk; green onion and scallion roots; chili seeds and stems
- **Why**: Lignin and insoluble fiber are too high for livestock to digest, and pungent compounds (allicin, capsaicin) make animals eat less of the feed.

### (4) Calcium shells and processing residue
- **Items**: chicken, quail and duck eggshells; herbal medicine residue; coffee grounds; tea bags (including the tea leaves)
- **Why**: Eggshells are calcium carbonate with no feed value, and tea bags contain paper or nylon fibers that contaminate compost.

<Callout type="important" title="Three rules for food waste">
1. **Drain it**: Squeeze out soup and water as much as possible (less salt and lighter transport).
2. **Remove foreign objects**: Keep out plastic film, toothpicks, packaging and bottle caps completely.
3. **Cut it small**: Chop bulky vegetables and fruit, such as whole radishes, whole cabbages and watermelon rinds, into small pieces.
</Callout>

Item rules differ slightly by local ordinance. The table below shows the common standard, so also check your city or district's guide.

| Item | Correct channel | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Watermelon, melon and banana peels** | **Food waste** | Soft, easy to shred, can become feed or compost (cut watermelon rind small) |
| **Apple, pear and orange peels** | **Food waste** | Rich in organic matter, good for composting |
| **Pineapple peel and core** | **Regular bag** | Fibers are very tough and tangle the shredder |
| **Onion and garlic skins** | **Regular bag** | Indigestible fiber and pungent compounds |
| **Eggshells and clam shells** | **Regular bag** | Calcium carbonate (no feed value, damages machines) |
| **Chicken bones and rib bones** | **Regular bag** | Can't be shredded and can injure livestock |

## Summary

Ask yourself, "Could a dog, or a farm's cows and pigs, eat this?" and most food waste decisions become easy.

Anything hard or tough, such as fibrous peels, shells and bones, goes in the **regular bag**. Chapter 4 covers the correct routes for **gel ice packs and unused medicine**, which threaten soil and water ecosystems.
