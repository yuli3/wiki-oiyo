<HighlightBox type="abstract" title="Definition">
  **Blood Type B personality** is a popular folk stereotype in Korea and Japan,
  where Type B people are often described as free-spirited, individualistic,
  direct, or creative. This is folklore and entertainment only: there is **no
  reliable scientific evidence** that ABO blood type predicts personality.
</HighlightBox>

## The folk stereotype

The Type B stereotype says that people with Type B blood are independent,
curious, expressive, and less bound by convention. In popular media, Type B may
be imagined as the person who follows personal interest, resists pressure to
conform, speaks frankly, or brings unusual energy to a group.

The stereotype also has a negative version. Type B is sometimes unfairly
portrayed as selfish, unreliable, moody, or difficult in relationships. Those
claims are part of the cultural script, not facts about people with Type B
blood. A person's habits, values, maturity, and behavior cannot be read from
ABO type.

Because the Type B image can be more judgmental than playful, it needs special
care. Saying "that is so Type B" may sound casual, but it can quickly become a
shortcut for dismissing someone's boundaries, creativity, or directness.

## Where it comes from

Blood-type personality folklore developed in modern Japan, separate from the
medical discovery of ABO blood groups. In the 1920s, Takeji Furukawa wrote
about possible links between blood type and temperament, but these ideas were
not established as scientific psychology.

Later, popular writers and media revived the theme. Masahiko Nomi's books in
the 1970s helped turn blood-type personality into a mass-market topic. Korean
popular culture then used the idea in comedy, dating talk, celebrity content,
and everyday conversation. The broader framework is summarized in the
[blood-type personality definition](/en/meaning-of-blood-type/).

## Why it is not science

Reliable personality science requires careful measurement, replication, and
evidence that a claim works beyond coincidence and stereotype. Large studies
have not shown a dependable link between ABO blood type and personality. Kengo
Nawata's large-scale analyses in Japan and the United States, along with other
critical studies, do not support the folk theory.

The Type B stereotype may seem persuasive because of confirmation bias. People
notice the independent Type B acquaintance and forget the cautious or quiet
ones. Media repetition also teaches people which traits to expect.

The risk is not only inaccuracy. Blood-type labeling can support social bias,
including **bura-hara**, or blood-type harassment, in Japan. It can affect
dating assumptions, teasing, hiring impressions, or group exclusion.

Blood Type B personality is entertainment and cultural folklore only. It is not
science, psychology, diagnosis, or a valid basis for judging people, hiring
them, excluding them, dating them, or predicting relationship success.
