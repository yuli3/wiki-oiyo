## 1. Origin: watching behavior, not emotion

In 1928, psychologist William Marston argued in *Emotions of Normal People* that behavior varies with how we perceive our environment (favorable/antagonistic) and ourselves (strong/weak). The combination of these two axes became today's four DISC types. Where MBTI addresses inner "preferences," DISC focuses on observable "behavior."

## 2. The four types

- **D (Dominance):** Values results and speed. Direct and challenging, energized by control and achievement.
- **I (Influence):** Values people and mood. Enthusiastic and optimistic, energized by relationships and recognition.
- **S (Steadiness):** Values harmony and consistency. Calm and cooperative, reassured by predictability and trust.
- **C (Conscientiousness):** Values accuracy and principle. Analytical and careful, confident with data and quality.

Most people show a blended profile of two or three types rather than a single fixed one.

## 3. Where it is used

DISC is not a clinical diagnostic tool but a **language for communication and collaboration**. It is used in team building, leadership coaching, and sales/customer service to train "how to speak to someone's behavioral style." The point is not to label others, but to respect ways of working different from your own.

## 4. Limits and proper use

Because DISC describes behavior that shifts with situation, it should not be mistaken for an "unchanging essence." Using it to decide hiring outcomes or a person's worth is inappropriate. It is most useful as a starting point for self-understanding and mutual respect.

### Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

**Q: How is DISC different from MBTI?**
A: MBTI looks at inner "preferences" for taking in and judging information; DISC looks at outward "behavioral tendencies." DISC is more influenced by situation and environment.

**Q: Do the types change?**
A: Core tendencies are relatively stable, but the behavior you show outwardly can shift with role, stress, and environment.
