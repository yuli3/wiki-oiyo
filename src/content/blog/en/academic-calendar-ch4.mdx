In South Korea, entry into elementary school marks the beginning of the nation's 9-year **compulsory education mandate** (6 years of primary school and 3 years of middle school, guaranteed free of tuition under the Constitution).

The legal eligibility criteria for first-grade matriculation are defined strictly by birth dates under administrative education regulations.

---

## 1. Statutory Cohort Formula: The January 1 to December 31 Rule

**Article 13(1) of the Elementary and Secondary Education Act** places the duty on parents and guardians: they must enroll their child in elementary school on March 1 of the year after the year in which the child turns six. The duty of the head of each Eup/Myeon/Dong office to notify families of school entry sits in the Enforcement Decree, not in the Act. The exact wording is on the [Korean Law Information Center](https://www.law.go.kr/법령/초·중등교육법).

In South Korea, school cohorts are organized strictly by **Calendar Birth Year**:
- A child born anytime between **January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2019**, turns 6 years old in 2025 and officially enters elementary school on **March 1, 2026**.
- All children born in the same calendar year matriculate together into the same primary school cohort, regardless of their specific birth month.

---

## 2. Historical Abolition of the "Fast Birthday" (Pparun Saengil) System

Prior to 2009, South Korea utilized a cohort cut-off that matched the academic year: children born in January and February entered school alongside children born the preceding calendar year (known culturally as *Pparun Saengil*, or "fast birthdays").

This created significant social and developmental dilemmas:
1. **Developmental Physical Gaps**: A child born in late February 1990 sat in the same classroom as a child born in March 1989—a physical age gap of up to 11 to 12 months. In early primary grades, this created large disparities in motor coordination, stamina, reading fluency, and cognitive concentration.
2. **Social Hierarchical Confusion**: In Korean culture, social relationships and honorific speech (*Jondaetmal*) are strictly organized around chronological age. Fast-birthday students frequently encountered social friction when entering university or corporate workplaces, where cohort classmates were an entire year older.

Consequently, by Education Law Amendment in 2008 (enacted for students matriculating in 2009), the Ministry of Education **completely abolished the automatic early entry of January-February births**, aligning compulsory school cohorts exclusively to standard January 1st through December 31st calendar birth years.

---

## 3. Statutory Deferral and Early Entry Applications

Parents may still exercise statutory options under municipal procedures:

- **Enrollment Deferral (*Chwihak Yuye*)**: If a child experiences developmental delays, medical treatment, or speech therapy, parents may submit a formal Deferral Application to the local community center (*Dong Office*) between October 1 and December 31. This postpones school entry by exactly one year without legal penalty.
- **Voluntary Early Entry (*Jogi Iphak*)**: Parents who determine their child demonstrates advanced readiness may voluntarily request early matriculation one year ahead of schedule. However, once submitted and matriculated, early entry cannot be retroactively reversed.
