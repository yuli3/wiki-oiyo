**A phase diagram is a map: for a given temperature and composition it tells you which phases exist at equilibrium, what each one contains, and how much of each there is.** Chapter 4 computed how fast atoms move. This chapter covers the **equilibrium** they reach when given enough time.

The diagram values below belong to a **hypothetical A–B binary system** chosen for practice. They are not data for any real alloy.

---

## 1. What you read: phase, composition, amount

- **Phase**: a region with uniform structure and composition, such as liquid L or solid solution α.
- **Composition**: how many wt% B each phase contains.
- **Amount (phase fraction)**: the share of total mass in each phase.

The overall alloy composition $C_0$ differs from the composition of each phase. Mixing them up is the most common misreading.

---

## 2. The hypothetical A–B system: complete solid solubility

Assume A and B mix in any ratio even as solids. A melts at 1500 K, B at 1100 K. Above the upper curve (the **liquidus**) everything is liquid, below the lower curve (the **solidus**) everything is solid α, and between them lies the two-phase L + α field.

The compositions where each temperature meets the two curves:

| Temperature (K) | Solidus $C_\alpha$ (wt% B) | Liquidus $C_L$ (wt% B) |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1350 | 12 | 26 |
| 1300 | 20 | 35 |
| 1250 | 29 | 45 |

---

## 3. Tie lines: read phase compositions at the ends

A horizontal segment through a point in the two-phase field, ending at both boundaries, is a **tie line**. Whatever the overall composition, at that temperature **the liquid has the liquidus-end composition and the solid has the solidus-end composition**.

At 1300 K, whether $C_0$ is 21 or 34 wt% B, the liquid holds 35 wt% B and α holds 20 wt% B. Only the **amounts** change.

The **Gibbs phase rule** explains this. At constant pressure, $F=C-P+1$. With $C=2$ components and $P=2$ phases, $F=1$: choosing the temperature fixes both phase compositions.

---

## 4. The lever rule: amount scales with the opposite arm

Treat the tie line as a lever with the alloy composition $C_0$ as the fulcrum:

$$W_L=\frac{C_0-C_\alpha}{C_L-C_\alpha},\qquad W_\alpha=\frac{C_L-C_0}{C_L-C_\alpha}$$

Each phase fraction is proportional to the arm **on the far side from that phase**. The two fractions sum to 1.

For $C_0=30$ wt% B at 1300 K:

$$W_L=\frac{30-20}{35-20}=\frac{10}{15}\approx0.667,\qquad W_\alpha=\frac{35-30}{15}\approx0.333$$

**Check with a mass balance.** In 1 kg of alloy, 0.667 kg of liquid holds $0.667\times0.35=0.233$ kg B and 0.333 kg of solid holds $0.333\times0.20=0.067$ kg B, for 0.300 kg in total: the 30 wt% B we started with.

---

## 5. Following an alloy as it cools

The same $C_0=30$ wt% B alloy, cooled slowly, at three temperatures:

| Temperature (K) | Reading | $W_L$ | $W_\alpha$ | Solid composition (wt% B) |
|---:|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1350 | $C_0=30>C_L=26$: single-phase liquid | 1 | 0 | — |
| 1300 | two-phase | 0.667 | 0.333 | 20 |
| 1250 | two-phase | $(30-29)/(45-29)\approx0.063$ | 0.938 | 29 |

At 1350 K the alloy has more B than the liquidus composition, so it is still in the liquid field. As temperature falls the solid grows, and **the first solid is A-rich (20 wt% B) while later solid carries more B.** At equilibrium, diffusion evens out the whole solid, which ends at 30 wt% B once fully solidified.

---

## 6. What not to do with this model

- **Do not treat real cooling as equilibrium.** As Chapter 4 showed, solid-state diffusion is slow; fast cooling leaves **coring**, where the first-frozen centre and last-frozen rim differ in composition.
- **Do not assume every binary is isomorphous.** Systems with eutectics, peritectics or intermetallic compounds have different fields, and along a three-phase horizontal line the degrees of freedom are zero.
- **Do not apply the hypothetical values to real alloys.** Melting points and compositions are for practice; real diagrams come from measurement and thermodynamic databases.
