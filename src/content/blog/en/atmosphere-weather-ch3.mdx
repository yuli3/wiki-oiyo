Thermometers measure dry-bulb kinetic air temperature. However, living biological organisms do not experience raw air temperature in a vacuum. Instead, humans sense the **net rate of heat flux leaving or entering the dermal boundary layer**.

The **Perceived Temperature** (sensible temperature) models this dynamic equilibrium, dividing into two complementary seasonal regimes: **Wind Chill Index** in winter and **Heat Index** in summer.

## 1. Winter Wind Chill: The JAG/TI Convective Model

In freezing weather, resting human skin maintains a microscopic microclimate: an insulating, motionless boundary layer of air heated by dermal metabolic perfusion. When winter winds blow, turbulent air currents strip this thermal cushion away, dramatically accelerating convective heat loss.

The Korea Meteorological Administration (KMA), the U.S. National Weather Service (NWS), and Environment Canada officially calculate winter wind chill using the **JAG/TI (Joint Action Group for Temperature Indices) empirical formula**:

$$Twc = 13.12 + 0.6215\,T - 11.37\,V^{0.16} + 0.3965\,T\,V^{0.16}$$

Where:
- $Twc$: Wind chill equivalent temperature (°C)
- $T$: Ambient dry-bulb air temperature (°C, validated for $T \le 10^\circ C$)
- $V$: Wind velocity measured at standard anemometer height of 10 meters ($km/h$, validated for $V \ge 4.8\,km/h$)

The non-linear exponent $0.16$ represents empirical skin surface heat transfer mechanics derived from twelve human clinical trials. At low wind speeds, slight gusts cause massive sensible temperature drops; at higher speeds, the cooling curve levels off.

## 2. Summer Heat Index: Evaporative Cooling Breakdown

In hot weather ($T \ge 27^\circ C$), ambient air often exceeds skin temperature ($33^\circ C$). Under these conditions, the body can no longer shed heat via radiation or convection. The sole physiological mechanism available to prevent core hyperthermia is **sweat evaporation**.

The latent heat of vaporization of water removes approximately 2.4 kilojoules per gram of sweat evaporated. However, when ambient relative humidity ($RH$) is elevated:
1. High atmospheric vapor pressure suppresses the dermal evaporation rate.
2. Sweat beads up and drips off without cooling the skin.
3. Core body temperature climbs, causing heat exhaustion or fatal heatstroke.
