<HighlightBox type="abstract" title="Definition">
  **Blood Type O personality** is a popular folk stereotype in Korea and Japan,
  where Type O people are often described as sociable, confident, practical, and
  leadership-oriented. This is cultural folklore and entertainment only: ABO
  blood type has **no reliable scientific support** as a personality measure.
</HighlightBox>

## The folk stereotype

The Type O stereotype says that people with Type O blood are outgoing,
energetic, confident, and comfortable taking charge. In everyday pop-culture
language, Type O may be imagined as the social connector, the practical
organizer, the bold friend, or the person who can keep a group moving.

The stereotype also has a shadow side. Some versions describe Type O as
competitive, blunt, attention-seeking, or overly confident. These are
popularly believed associations, not biological facts. A Type O person may be
introverted, cautious, sensitive, quiet, analytical, artistic, or any other
combination of traits.

The safest interpretation is symbolic: Type O represents a folk image of
confidence and social drive. It does not reveal actual leadership ability,
emotional intelligence, ethics, competence, or compatibility.

## Where it comes from

The ABO blood group system is medically real and important, but personality
claims about ABO type are a separate modern folklore tradition. In early
20th-century Japan, Takeji Furukawa proposed links between blood type and
temperament. Those claims did not become accepted science.

The belief later became widely familiar through mass-market books, magazines,
television, manga, and celebrity profiles, especially after Masahiko Nomi's
1970s publications. Korea adopted the framework as a familiar entertainment
topic and social shorthand. The general history is covered in the
[blood-type personality definition](/en/meaning-of-blood-type/).

## Why it is not science

Large studies have not found a reliable link between ABO blood type and
personality. Kengo Nawata's research using large survey samples in Japan and
the United States found no meaningful relationship, and other studies have not
verified the popular four-type pattern.

What makes the Type O stereotype feel plausible is not strong evidence, but
ordinary human pattern-making. Broad descriptions fit many people. People
remember examples that match the label and overlook counterexamples. Social
expectations can also encourage people to perform the role they have been told
belongs to them.

Blood-type labeling may lead to bias or discrimination. In Japan, **bura-hara**
refers to blood-type harassment, where people are judged or pressured because
of their ABO type.

Blood Type O personality is entertainment and cultural folklore only. It is not
science, psychology, diagnosis, or a basis for judging people, hiring,
leadership selection, dating, marriage, friendship, or serious life decisions.
