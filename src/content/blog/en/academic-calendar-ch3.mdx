South Korea's March start and Japan's April start do not, by themselves, tell you the first day of attendance or a transferring child's grade. Legal school years, first teaching days and age cut-offs answer different questions.

This chapter starts with South Korean primary and secondary education and compares Japanese and UK examples. It is not an admissions guide for universities or international schools. Official material was checked on 21 October 2026.

## 1. Three different meanings of a start date

<dl>
<dt>Legal school-year boundary</dt>
<dd>The beginning of an annual institutional period, not necessarily a day students attend.</dd>
<dt>First teaching day</dt>
<dd>The date students begin classes in the school calendar. Staff preparation days and entrance ceremonies may be different dates.</dd>
<dt>Admission and grade-placement rule</dt>
<dd>The age or educational requirements for placement. These cannot be converted from the starting month alone.</dd>
</dl>

## 2. Korea and Japan: compare legal boundaries

[Article 24 of Korea's Elementary and Secondary Education Act](https://law.go.kr/LSW/lsLinkCommonInfo.do?chrClsCd=010202&lspttninfSeq=147073) sets the school year from 1 March to the end of the following February. [Japan's education ministry](https://www.mext.go.jp/a_menu/shotou/shugaku/detail/1422233.htm) explains that the elementary school year runs from 1 April to 31 March. These are legal boundaries, not confirmed ceremony or first-attendance dates at an individual school.

A school year is also not a term. Term divisions and closure dates are separate scheduling questions. A one-month difference between legal starts does not establish a one-month transfer gap or guarantee the same grade for children of the same age.

## 3. Do not reduce the UK to a September rule

[GOV.UK's term-date service](https://www.gov.uk/school-term-holiday-dates) states that dates vary across the UK and directs families to local calendars. [Education Scotland](https://education.gov.scot/parentzone/my-school/general-school-information/term-dates) describes a usual start in the second or third week of August, with dates set by local authorities. That guidance was last updated on 6 June 2023; it is not evidence for a particular first day in 2026.

A country name is therefore a starting point for finding a calendar, not the calendar itself. Check the authority and school responsible for the relevant year. These primary/secondary examples also do not establish university exchange deadlines, module availability or credit-transfer rules.

<details>
<summary>The same starting month does not mean the same transfer timetable</summary>

Imagine two fictional schools both starting classes in September. One closes applications before summer; the other accepts applications during term. Their preparation schedules differ despite the shared month. This is an editorial example separating first teaching day from application deadline, not a statement about a real admissions process. The receiving school must confirm mid-year admission and documents.

</details>

## 4. A practical sequence for matching calendars

<ol>
<li>Ask the sending school for the final teaching date and when completion or results documents become available.</li>
<li>Identify the receiving jurisdiction, school level, public/private status and school-year calendar.</li>
<li>Confirm age cut-offs, prior-education requirements, grade placement and mid-year entry with admissions staff.</li>
<li>List application, document, ceremony and first-teaching dates separately, then calculate gaps or clashes.</li>
</ol>

Current calendars alone cannot prove that farming caused a worldwide September convention or heating constraints caused Korea's March start. Nor do they establish that reform inevitably creates a cohort 1.5 times larger or a particular transition cost. Those historical or policy claims need their own records and an explicit reform design.
