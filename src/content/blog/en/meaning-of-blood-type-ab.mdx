<HighlightBox type="abstract" title="Definition">
  **Blood Type AB personality** is a popular folk stereotype in Korea and
  Japan, where Type AB people are often described as rational, private,
  complex, or dual-natured. This is cultural folklore and entertainment only:
  ABO blood type has **no reliable scientific support** as a personality test.
</HighlightBox>

## The folk stereotype

The Type AB stereotype says that people with Type AB blood are analytical,
private, calm, unusual, and hard to categorize. In popular descriptions, Type
AB may be imagined as someone who observes before speaking, separates emotion
from decision-making, and moves between different social modes.

The common phrase "dual nature" is especially important to handle carefully.
The stereotype may describe Type AB as both rational and sensitive, distant and
caring, sociable and solitary. That language is folklore, not psychology. It
does not mean Type AB people have divided personalities, mental-health traits,
or predictable contradictions.

At most, the Type AB label is a cultural image of complexity and privacy. It
cannot tell you whether a person is trustworthy, emotionally available,
creative, compatible, or difficult.

## Where it comes from

Blood-type personality belief grew in modern Japan after ABO blood groups had
already become medically important. Takeji Furukawa wrote in the 1920s about
possible relationships between blood type and temperament, but the claims were
not accepted as reliable science.

The idea later became popular through books, magazines, television, manga, and
celebrity culture. Masahiko Nomi's 1970s publications were especially
influential in turning the four blood types into a familiar pop-psychology
framework. Korea adopted the idea as entertainment and social shorthand. See
the broader [blood-type personality definition](/en/meaning-of-blood-type/) for
the full context.

## Why it is not science

Large studies and critical reviews do not support ABO blood type as a reliable
predictor of personality. Kengo Nawata's large-scale survey research in Japan
and the United States found no meaningful relationship, and other studies have
not confirmed the folk typology.

The AB stereotype can feel convincing because "complex" is a very broad
description. Most people are different across contexts: work, family, private
life, stress, rest, and close relationships. That ordinary complexity does not
come from ABO blood type.

Blood-type claims can also create bias. In Japan, **bura-hara**, or blood-type
harassment, describes judgment, teasing, or exclusion based on a person's blood
type. The AB label may invite unfair assumptions that someone is strange,
cold, or hard to understand.

Blood Type AB personality is entertainment and cultural folklore only. It is
not science, psychology, diagnosis, or a basis for judging people, hiring,
dating, friendship, marriage, or mental-health interpretation.
