**Most of the difference between metals, semiconductors and insulators comes down to one gap between allowed energy bands: the band gap.** Vacancies in Chapter 3 and diffusion in Chapter 4 followed $\exp(-Q/kT)$. Charge carriers in a semiconductor follow the same form, and this chapter uses it to show why doping is so powerful.

The band gap, mobilities and concentrations below belong to a **hypothetical semiconductor**. They are close to silicon in order of magnitude but are not silicon data.

---

## 1. Bands and the gap

Electron energies in a solid group into continuous **bands**. The highest filled band is the **valence band**, the empty band above it is the **conduction band**, and the gap between them is the **band gap $E_g$**.

- **Metals**: bands are partly filled or overlap, so electrons move under a tiny field.
- **Semiconductors**: $E_g$ is a few eV or less, so heat lifts some electrons into the conduction band.
- **Insulators**: $E_g$ is large and practically no electrons are lifted at room temperature.

A lifted electron leaves an empty state, a **hole**, in the valence band, and holes carry current too.

---

## 2. Intrinsic carrier concentration is temperature-sensitive

In a pure **intrinsic semiconductor** the electron concentration n equals the hole concentration p, and their common value $n_i$ is approximately

$$n_i\propto T^{3/2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{E_g}{2kT}\right)$$

Unlike Chapters 3 and 4, **half** of $E_g$ appears in the exponent. For the hypothetical semiconductor take $E_g=1.10$ eV and $n_i(300\text{ K})=1.0\times10^{10}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$, and scale by ratio (ignoring the temperature change of $E_g$).

| Temperature (K) | $E_g/2kT$ | $n_i$ (cm⁻³) |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 300 | 21.27 | 1.0 × 10¹⁰ |
| 350 | 18.24 | 2.6 × 10¹¹ |
| 400 | 15.96 | 3.1 × 10¹² |
| 500 | 12.76 | 1.1 × 10¹⁴ |

The 400 K row is $(400/300)^{1.5}\times\exp[21.27-15.96]=1.54\times204\approx314$ times the 300 K value. A 100 K rise multiplies $n_i$ by more than 300, so an intrinsic semiconductor alone swings strongly with temperature.

---

## 3. Doping and the mass action law

Adding a **donor** that gives up one extra electron (such as a group-15 element) at $N_D=1.0\times10^{16}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$, about one atom in several million, ionises almost fully at room temperature and gives $n\approx N_D$. This is an **n-type** semiconductor.

At equilibrium, $n\cdot p=n_i^2$ holds regardless of doping (the mass action law). So

$$p=\frac{n_i^2}{n}=\frac{(1.0\times10^{10})^2}{1.0\times10^{16}}=1.0\times10^{4}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$$

Electrons become the majority carriers and holes the minority. Even at 500 K, $n_i=1.1\times10^{14}$ is 100 times smaller than $N_D$, so across this range n stays at $N_D$ almost independently of temperature. That is why doping **stabilises** the material.

---

## 4. Conductivity: σ = n·q·μ

Conductivity is the product of carrier number, charge $q=1.602\times10^{-19}$ C and **mobility μ** (drift speed per 1 V/cm). Use hypothetical $\mu_n=1350$ and $\mu_p=480\ \text{cm}^2/\text{V·s}$.

| Case | Calculation | σ (S/cm) |
|---|---|---:|
| Intrinsic (300 K) | $n_i q(\mu_n+\mu_p)=10^{10}\times1.602\times10^{-19}\times1830$ | 2.9 × 10⁻⁶ |
| n-type $N_D=10^{16}$ | $N_D q\mu_n=10^{16}\times1.602\times10^{-19}\times1350$ | 2.16 |

The ratio is about **7.4 × 10⁵**. An impurity at less than one atom per million changes conductivity 740,000-fold. The n-type resistivity is $1/2.16\approx0.46\ \Omega\cdot\text{cm}$.

---

## 5. Light and insulators with the same equations

- **Absorption edge**: only photons with more energy than the gap lift electrons. $\lambda=hc/E_g\approx1240/E_g$ nm, so $E_g=1.10$ eV absorbs light shorter than about **1127 nm**. This sets the sensitivity range of solar cells and photodetectors.
- **Insulator comparison**: for $E_g=5.5$ eV with the same prefactor, the $n_i$ ratio at 300 K is $\exp[-(5.5-1.1)/(2kT)]\approx10^{-37}$. Practically no electrons reach the conduction band at room temperature. Such a material absorbs only ultraviolet below about 225 nm, so it looks transparent to the eye.

---

## 6. What not to do with this model

- **Do not treat the $n_i$ prefactor as constant.** Effective masses and $E_g$ itself change with temperature; use measured tables for real values.
- **Do not assume donors are always fully ionised.** At very low temperature ionisation is incomplete; at very high temperature $n_i$ exceeds $N_D$ and the material turns intrinsic again.
- **Do not treat mobility as independent of doping.** More impurities mean more scattering and lower mobility.
- **Do not use the hypothetical values for real device design.**
