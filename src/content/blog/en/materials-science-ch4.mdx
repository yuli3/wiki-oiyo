**Atoms in a solid move too, and their speed changes by hundreds of times when the temperature rises only a little.** Chapter 3 showed the number of vacancies growing exponentially with temperature. This chapter covers **diffusion**, in which atoms travel by way of those vacancies. Heat treatment, surface hardening and semiconductor doping all rest on this calculation.

The assumptions match the earlier chapters. The diffusion constants and concentrations below are **hypothetical values** chosen to show the relationships. They are not data for any real metal or process.

---

## 1. Vacancy diffusion: an atom jumps into a neighbouring vacancy

When an atom in a crystal swaps places with a neighbouring vacancy, the atom moves one step and the vacancy moves one step the other way. This is **vacancy diffusion**. Each jump needs two things at once.

1. There must be a vacancy next to the atom. Its probability follows $\exp(-Q_v/kT)$ from Chapter 3.
2. The atom must clear the energy barrier $Q_m$ (the migration energy) to squeeze between its neighbours. That probability also has the form $\exp(-Q_m/kT)$.

Multiplying the two probabilities adds the exponents, so the **activation energy for vacancy diffusion is roughly $Q_d\approx Q_v+Q_m$**. In this chapter we add $Q_m=0.90$ eV to the Chapter 3 value $Q_v=0.90$ eV and set $Q_d=1.8$ eV.

**Interstitial diffusion**, in which small atoms move through the gaps between lattice sites, needs no vacancy and is usually much faster. This chapter calculates vacancy diffusion only.

---

## 2. Fick's first law: a concentration difference drives a flow

The amount passing per unit area per unit time from high to low concentration is the **diffusion flux J**. At steady state, when the concentration profile does not change with time,

$$J=-D\,\frac{dC}{dx}$$

D is the **diffusion coefficient** (m²/s) and $dC/dx$ is the concentration gradient. The minus sign means the flow goes toward lower concentration. For the same concentration difference, a larger D means more flow, so the properties of the material and the temperature are all contained in D.

---

## 3. Temperature dependence of the diffusion coefficient

$$D=D_0\exp\!\left(-\frac{Q_d}{kT}\right)$$

$D_0$ is a prefactor that barely depends on temperature. For the hypothetical element we take $D_0=2.0\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}$ and $Q_d=1.8$ eV ($k=8.617\times10^{-5}$ eV/K).

For a plate 5 mm thick with 1.2 kg/m³ on one side and 0.4 kg/m³ on the other, and a constant gradient, the flux magnitude is $J=D\times0.8/0.005$.

| Temperature (K) | $Q_d/kT$ | D (m²/s) | J (kg/m²·s) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 300 | 69.63 | 1.2 × 10⁻³⁵ | 1.8 × 10⁻³³ |
| 900 | 23.21 | 1.67 × 10⁻¹⁵ | 2.7 × 10⁻¹³ |
| 1200 | 17.41 | 5.51 × 10⁻¹³ | 8.8 × 10⁻¹¹ |

Working the 1200 K row by hand: $Q_d/kT=1.8/(8.617\times10^{-5}\times1200)=1.8/0.1034\approx17.41$, $e^{-17.41}\approx2.76\times10^{-8}$, and $D=2.0\times10^{-5}\times2.76\times10^{-8}\approx5.51\times10^{-13}$ m²/s. This uses the same exponent (17.41) as the Chapter 3 vacancy fraction at 600 K, because $Q_d$ is twice $Q_v$, so $Q_d/kT$ at 1200 K equals $Q_v/kT$ at 600 K.

While the temperature rose by a third from 900 K to 1200 K, D grew about 331 times. At room temperature, 300 K, it is effectively zero, which is why the composition of a metal part does not mix by itself at room temperature.

---

## 4. How long does it take: the characteristic length √(Dt)

When the concentration changes with time (Fick's second law), the solutions take several forms, but the length scale that appears in all of them is $\sqrt{Dt}$. Read it as the **rough size** of the distance diffusion reaches in time t. It is not an exact penetration depth; it gives the order of magnitude.

| Temperature (K) | $\sqrt{Dt}$ after 1 hour | Time to reach 100 µm ($t=x^2/D$) |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 900 | about 2.4 µm | about 70 days |
| 1200 | about 45 µm | about 5.0 hours |

At 1200 K, $t=(100\times10^{-6})^2/5.51\times10^{-13}\approx1.81\times10^{4}$ s ≈ 5.0 hours. The same 100 µm takes about 70 days at 900 K. The table shows why raising the heat-treatment temperature a little shortens process time a lot. The $x^2$ also shows directly that doubling the distance takes four times as long.

---

## 5. Working backwards: $Q_d$ from D at two temperatures

The method is the same as in Chapter 3. Since $\ln D=\ln D_0-\dfrac{Q_d}{k}\cdot\dfrac1T$,

$$Q_d=\frac{k\,\ln(D_2/D_1)}{1/T_1-1/T_2}$$

With the 900 K and 1200 K values: $D_2/D_1\approx331$, $\ln331\approx5.80$, $1/900-1/1200=2.78\times10^{-4}\ \text{K}^{-1}$, so $Q_d\approx8.617\times10^{-5}\times5.80/2.78\times10^{-4}\approx1.8$ eV, the value we assumed. Real experiments also measure D at several temperatures and take the activation energy from the slope of an Arrhenius plot.

---

## 6. What not to do with this model

- **Do not use √(Dt) as an exact penetration depth.** The real concentration profile takes forms such as the error function depending on the boundary conditions, and the prefactor depends on which concentration you use as the threshold.
- **Do not treat all diffusion as vacancy diffusion.** Interstitial diffusion of small atoms such as carbon and hydrogen, and fast paths along grain boundaries or surfaces, have different activation energies.
- **Do not use $Q_d=Q_v+Q_m$ as an exact equality.** It is an approximation; the real value is set by measurement.
- **Do not plug the hypothetical values into a real process.** The $D_0$, $Q_d$ and concentrations in the tables are for practice.
