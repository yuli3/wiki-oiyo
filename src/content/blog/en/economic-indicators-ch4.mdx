On many days the Korean market sets its course by US releases that come out in the early hours of the morning, Korean time. This chapter explains why US rates and data move Korean rates and the exchange rate, and what to look at first.

---

## 1. Who decides at the FOMC, and how often

The **target range for the federal funds rate**, the US counterpart of a base rate, is set by the Federal Reserve's **Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC)**.

- **12 voting members**: the 7 members of the Board of Governors + the president of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York + 4 of the remaining 11 regional Reserve Bank presidents (rotating every year)
- **8 scheduled meetings a year**: roughly every 6 weeks. The statement is released at 2 p.m. US Eastern time on the day the meeting ends, and the Chair's press conference starts 30 minutes later. In Korea this is the early morning of the next day.
- **Announced as a target range**: unlike Korea's single number, the rate is set as a range 0.25 percentage points wide, such as "4.25–4.50%."
- **Inflation target**: **2%** inflation in personal consumption expenditures (**PCE**) prices.

---

## 2. The dot plot

The March, June, September and December meetings also publish the **Summary of Economic Projections (SEP)**. The dot plot inside it shows each participant's view of the appropriate year-end rate as a single dot.

- The **median** of the dots is quoted as the reference for "how many more times the Fed will cut this year."
- The dot plot is not a promise but **a forecast at that point in time**. It often changes in the next quarter's dot plot.
- Changes in the statement's wording (for example, "the extent and timing of additional adjustments"), the number of dissents and the Chair's press conference matter as much as the dot plot.

---

## 3. Three US inflation and jobs indicators

| Indicator | Publisher | Frequency | How to read it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consumer Price Index (CPI) | Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) | Mid-month | The market reacts to it first. Read core CPI, excluding food and energy, alongside it |
| Personal consumption expenditures (PCE) prices | Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) | End of month | **The Fed's official target measure**. Broader in scope than CPI and usually comes in slightly lower |
| Nonfarm Payrolls | BLS "Employment Situation" report | Usually the first Friday of the month | Read job gains, the unemployment rate and hourly earnings together. Also check revisions to the previous two months |

When US CPI comes in higher than expected, it is often read as "rate cuts will be delayed," and US Treasury yields and the dollar tend to rise. When jobs come in much weaker than expected, the opposite happens. Here too, the key is **the gap from expectations**.

## 4. How US rates reach Korea

```
US data → change in FOMC expectations
  └─> US Treasury yields, dollar value
        ├─> Won/dollar exchange rate (Korea–US rate gap, foreign capital flows)
        ├─> Korean government bond yields (tend to move together)
        └─> The Bank of Korea's room to act (hard to rush a cut when exchange rate pressure is high)
```

- **The Korea–US rate gap**: when US rates are higher than Korea's, dollar assets become more attractive, creating pressure for a weaker won (a higher exchange rate). The exchange rate also depends heavily on the trade balance, risk appetite and supply and demand, so the rate gap alone does not explain it.
- **Import prices**: when the exchange rate rises, import prices for crude oil and raw materials rise, which feeds into the consumer prices covered in Chapter 2.
- **The Bank of Korea's statement**: the sentences on "exchange rate volatility" and "monetary policy in major economies" in the statement covered in Chapter 1 describe this path.

<Callout type="important" title="Checklist for the week">
- Start of the month: US Employment Situation report (Friday), Korean consumer price release
- Middle of the month: US CPI, Korean employment release
- End of the month: US PCE
- FOMC week: statement, dot plot (quarter-end meetings), Chair's press conference, then the next morning's reaction in Korean rates and the exchange rate
</Callout>

## Summary

The FOMC sets the target range for the federal funds rate 8 times a year and shows its outlook each quarter through the dot plot. US CPI, PCE and nonfarm payrolls change expectations for the FOMC, and those expectations pass through US rates and the dollar to the won/dollar exchange rate, Korean interest rates and the Bank of Korea's choices. Put the four chapters together and you get a single map: the base rate (Chapter 1) ← prices (Chapter 2) and employment (Chapter 3) ← US rates and the exchange rate (Chapter 4).
